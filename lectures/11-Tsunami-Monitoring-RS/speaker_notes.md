## 2. Use satellites and ocean sensors to detect a tsunami, warn the coast in time, and map the flooding and damage it leaves.

Remote sensing helps at every stage: elevation data for hazard maps before, ocean and space sensors during, and optical and radar images after. Engineers use all three.

## 4. Tsunamis and why we monitor them

Part 1: Causes, wave physics, the seafloor source, India’s threat, and before–during–after.

## 5. What makes a tsunami

Because the whole water column moves, a tsunami carries enormous energy and keeps flowing inland for many minutes, unlike a wind wave that breaks and runs back. Earthquakes cause about four out of five tsunamis.

## 6. Fast in deep water, high near the coast

Tsunamis are shallow-water waves everywhere, because even 4000 m of ocean is shallow compared with a wavelength of hundreds of kilometres. So their speed depends only on depth. Green's law says height is proportional to depth to the power minus one quarter, as long as energy is conserved and the wave does not break. Ships at sea barely notice a tsunami.

## 7. The seafloor lifts the sea

The sea surface rises with the seafloor because water cannot be compressed. In 2004, coasts east of the rupture, such as Thailand, saw the sea withdraw first; India and Sri Lanka, to the west, saw a crest first. Withdrawal of the sea is a natural warning sign.

## 8. India’s tsunami threat

Official Indian figures for 2004 were 10,749 dead and 5,640 missing, most in Tamil Nadu and the Andaman and Nicobar Islands. In 1945 a Makran earthquake of about magnitude 8.1 caused a tsunami with run-up of up to about 11 m in the Gulf of Kachchh and 2 m at Mumbai, where 15 people were washed away. India's centre also serves as a Regional Tsunami Service Provider for the Indian Ocean.

## 11. The warning chain

Speed matters more than precision for the first message. The timings shown are typical, not fixed: a centre close to a great earthquake must decide within minutes. INCOIS has operated since October 2007 and warns other Indian Ocean countries as well as India.

## 12. Measuring a great earthquake quickly

Each step of one in magnitude is about 32 times more energy, so 7.9 to 9.0 is about 45 times. Strictly, JMA's first magnitude is on its own scale, not moment magnitude, but the lesson is the same. The Japan Meteorological Agency used GPS buoy data to upgrade its warnings. Since 2011, real-time GNSS has been added to warning systems in Japan, the US and elsewhere. Source: Ozaki (2011), Earth, Planets and Space.

## 13. Sensors on the sea floor

DART stands for Deep-ocean Assessment and Reporting of Tsunamis. The sensor normally reports every 15 minutes and switches to rapid reporting when it detects a wave. Buoys are expensive to maintain, and vandalism and storms have left gaps in several networks, including Indonesia's in 2018.

## 14. Satellite altimeters

A radar altimeter measures the time for a pulse to bounce off the sea surface, giving sea-surface height to a few centimetres. Altimeter tsunami profiles are too rare to warn, but they are excellent for testing tsunami models. Sources: Gower (2005); Ablain et al. (2006), Geophysical Research Letters.

## 15. SWOT: a tsunami in two dimensions

SWOT (Surface Water and Ocean Topography) is a NASA and CNES mission launched in December 2022. It uses radar interferometry across a 120 km swath rather than a single nadir point. The trailing, dispersive waves it measured are not in the simple long-wave theory of this lecture, and they helped place the tsunami source within about 10 km of the trench.

## 16. Seeing a tsunami in the sky

The rising air disturbance changes the number of free electrons in the ionosphere, about 300 km up. GNSS signals are delayed by those electrons, so dual-frequency receivers can measure total electron content (TEC) along each line to a satellite. The method is still experimental: JPL says its outputs must be interpreted by trained experts and it is meant to add to, not replace, existing systems. Source: NASA JPL (September 2025).

## 17. A tsunami crossing the ocean

Animation computed from long-wave theory: the wave moves at the square root of g times depth, and its height follows Green's law. In the deep ocean it is a low, fast hump about 0.5 m high; a DART buoy and a satellite catch it on the way. On the continental slope and shelf it slows sharply and grows, reaching the tide gauge almost three hours after the earthquake. Heights are greatly exaggerated, and depths are drawn on a square-root scale so that the shelf can be seen.

## 18. How much time is there?

Travel-time maps like this are pre-computed for every likely source and stored, so that a warning centre can tell each coast when to expect the wave within minutes of an earthquake. The 2004 arrival times are from the EERI reconnaissance report (2005). The simulation here used a 4 km grid, a simple bathymetry and an Okada source; real warning models use detailed bathymetry and nested grids near the coast.

## 19. Mapping what it leaves behind

Part 3: Optical and radar damage mapping, inundation and run-up, and land that moved.

## 20. Optical images: before and after

Before-and-after images of Banda Aceh in 2004 became some of the best-known satellite images of any disaster. Cloud is the main limit: tsunamis often follow storms or happen in the monsoon, which is why radar is so useful. Copernicus Emergency Management Service rapid mapping also produces damage maps from these images.

## 21. Radar damage maps

A damage proxy map compares coherence between two images before the event with coherence across the event. Only places that were coherent before, such as buildings and roads, can show a loss. Vegetation and water are always incoherent, so they cannot be judged. Coherence was explained in Lectures 1 to 3.

## 22. Measuring how far and how high

Satellite images map the inundation limit; field teams then measure run-up and flow depth from water marks on walls, debris in trees and survey points. Together they test models and set design values. International survey teams visit after every large tsunami.

## 23. Land that moved

Uplift and subsidence also change tide-gauge records, so a gauge on land that sank shows an apparent rise in sea level. Sources: Meltzner et al. (2006), Journal of Geophysical Research, for the Andaman and Nicobar Islands; Geospatial Information Authority of Japan for Tōhoku.

## 24. Preparing for the next one

Part 4: Elevation models, hazard and evacuation maps, and Tsunami Ready communities.

## 25. Elevation is everything

On a gentle coastal plain, every metre of height error moves the inundation limit by hundreds of metres. The simple level test here ignores the fact that a real tsunami loses height as it flows inland; proper inundation models solve the flow over the land. India's coastal lidar and CartoDEM, and corrected products such as CoastalDEM, are better than raw global models.

## 26. Hazard maps and evacuation

Tsunami Ready is a UNESCO-IOC programme with a set of indicators covering assessment, preparedness and response, such as mapped hazard zones, displayed evacuation maps, public awareness and regular drills. The Odisha villages are also exposed to cyclones; the same maps and drills help with storm surges.

## 27. Case studies

Part 5: Indian Ocean 2004, Tōhoku 2011, Palu 2018 and Hunga Tonga 2022.

## 28. Indian Ocean, 26 December 2004

In India most deaths were in Tamil Nadu, especially Nagapattinam, and in the Andaman and Nicobar Islands. Chennai saw waves of about 3 m and Port Blair about 4.5 m. A warning system would have given the Indian mainland about two hours.

## 29. Tōhoku, Japan, 11 March 2011

Japan had the world's most advanced tsunami defences and warning system, yet many sea walls were overtopped. The event led to real-time GNSS in warnings and to designing for the largest possible event, not just the largest recorded.

## 30. Palu, Sulawesi, 28 September 2018

Most tsunami warnings assume a thrust earthquake that lifts the seafloor. Palu showed that strike-slip faults in bays, with landslides, can also be deadly. The arrival was so fast that the official warning could not help.

## 31. Hunga Tonga–Hunga Ha’apai, 15 January 2022

The atmospheric Lamb wave circled the Earth several times and was recorded by barometers and tide gauges worldwide. In Peru the waves also caused an oil spill at the La Pampilla refinery near Lima. Volcanic tsunamis are a reminder to monitor sea level everywhere, not only near known faults.

## 33. Where engineers use tsunami data

In 2004 the Kalpakkam nuclear plant near Chennai shut down safely; in 2011 flooding of Fukushima Daiichi's emergency power led to meltdowns. Chapter 6 of ASCE 7-16 was the first US standard for tsunami loads on buildings. Coastal forests and mangroves reduce flow speed for small and moderate tsunamis, but cannot stop the largest.

## 34. A tsunami hazard workflow

Steps 1 to 5 happen before any tsunami and are where most engineering effort goes. Step 6 is the warning centre's job. Steps 7 and 8 close the loop: every event teaches something that should change the maps and the plans.

## 35. Limits and good practice

Remote sensing is strongest before and after a tsunami. During the event, ground and ocean sensors do most of the work, with satellites (GNSS, altimetry, the ionosphere) adding new and growing capability.
