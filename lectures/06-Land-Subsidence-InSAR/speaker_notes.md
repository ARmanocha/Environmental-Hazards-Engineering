# Land Subsidence Monitoring with InSAR — speaker notes

## 1. Land Subsidence Monitoring with InSAR

Land subsidence is the most widespread ground-motion hazard that InSAR measures, and the one closest to everyday engineering: buildings, pipes, railways and flood defences. The strip at the bottom is a simulated interferogram: each set of rings is a sinking area.

## 2. Use satellite radar to find where the ground is sinking, explain why, and judge what it means for buildings, infrastructure and flooding.

Most subsidence today is caused by people, mainly by pumping groundwater. That makes it one of the few geohazards we can actually stop, if we measure it and act on it.

## 5. The ground is sinking

Subsidence is slow and silent: nobody notices a few centimetres a year until a building cracks, a sewer stops draining or a street floods at every high tide. That is exactly why a wide-area measurement like InSAR matters.

## 6. Why the ground sinks

Tunnelling and construction dewatering add local, short-lived settlement too. Of all these, groundwater pumping affects by far the most people, so the rest of the lecture focuses on it.

## 7. How pumping makes the ground sink

In this simulation the ground sinks about 53 cm in eight years of over-pumping, and recovers only about 3 cm when pumping is cut. The same physics explains why subsidence is mostly irreversible and why the aquifer permanently loses storage: the squeezed clay cannot hold the same volume of water again.

## 8. Pump, squeeze, sink

Animation: a pump lowers the water level; the clay layers squeeze and the houses sink with the ground surface. When pumping stops and the water level recovers, the ground rises only a little: most of the sinking is permanent. Vertical scale exaggerated.

## 9. Why InSAR is the right tool

Before InSAR, subsidence was measured by repeated levelling along a few survey lines, every few years. InSAR gives a whole map every 6 to 12 days.

## 10. Measuring subsidence with InSAR

Part 2: Vertical motion, two passes, techniques and ground checks.

## 11. Mostly vertical, but not only

The sign convention: here positive LOS means towards the satellite, so sinking ground gives negative values. Many published maps use the opposite convention; always check the legend.

## 12. Two passes: vertical and east–west

This is the same decomposition as in the landslide and earthquake lectures. Both passes must cover the same time period and be resampled to a common grid. EGMS in Europe provides exactly this as its ortho product.

## 13. Techniques and ground checks

A reliable subsidence study combines a long InSAR time series (both passes), GNSS for the reference frame, and groundwater levels from wells to explain what is seen. Choose the reference area on stable ground, such as bedrock outside the basin.

## 16. Time series: the ground follows the water

This pattern of falling water, sinking ground, and slowing after better management is what InSAR showed in parts of Delhi between 2014 and 2023 (case study in Part 4).

## 17. From sinking ground to lost groundwater

Hydrogeologists call these the elastic and inelastic skeletal storage coefficients. In Mexico City, InSAR studies conclude that most of the elevation and aquifer storage already lost will not come back (Chaussard et al., 2021).

## 18. What damages buildings: differential settlement

This is how the 2025 Nature Sustainability study of five Indian megacities estimated building damage risk: InSAR velocities converted into angular distortion and compared with damage thresholds. The same idea applies to pipelines, rail track and bridge joints.

## 19. Case studies

Part 4: Mexico City, Jakarta, Delhi, California’s Central Valley and the global picture.

## 20. Mexico City, Mexico

Mexico City is the classic example of extreme groundwater-driven subsidence. The city pumps much of its water from the aquifer beneath it, and the soft lake clays compact. Chaussard and colleagues titled their 2021 paper 'Over a century of sinking in Mexico City: no hope for significant elevation and storage capacity recovery'.

## 21. Jakarta, Indonesia

Jakarta shows the coastal version of the problem: land sinking ten times faster than the sea is rising. Flooding and sinking were among the reasons Indonesia gave for moving its capital.

## 22. Delhi National Capital Region, India

Delhi is a hopeful case study. Garg and colleagues (2022) used persistent-scatterer InSAR to reveal the hidden subsidence; Kumar and colleagues (2025) then showed the ground rebounding by 5 to 10 cm in Dwarka from 2016 as groundwater levels recovered by more than 1.5 m.

## 23. San Joaquin Valley, California

The famous photograph of Joseph Poland beside a pole marking 9 m of subsidence from 1925 to 1977 comes from here. In 2015 NASA used UAVSAR, ALOS and Sentinel-1 to show record rates during the drought.

## 24. Sinking cities worldwide

The 2025 study covered Delhi, Mumbai, Chennai, Kolkata and Bengaluru: more than 13 million buildings. Sustained for 50 years, current rates could put about 23,500 buildings at very high risk. Osaka sank by more than 2 m in places before strict groundwater rules in the early 1960s.

## 26. What subsidence does to infrastructure

Most of these are differential effects: the hazard is not how far the ground sinks, but how unevenly, and where a structure crosses the change.

## 27. Slowing and stopping subsidence

Design measures also help: flexible pipe joints, foundations on deeper firm layers, and avoiding sensitive structures where the rate changes sharply. But only managing the groundwater removes the cause.

## 28. Limits and good practice

A building with no InSAR point on it is not necessarily stable; it may just not reflect well. Use the nearest points with care and check on site.
